Cambridge AS & A Level IT · Chapter 14 Communications Technology

Wireless & Mobile

Connecting without wires: Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, infrared and microwave links, and how cellular networks, satellites and GPS let mobile devices communicate on the move.

Wireless technology 14.10

Wi-Fi

Wi-Fi uses radio signals, and is the most widely used method of wireless communication. Each device wanting to connect broadcasts a radio signal, which a router or WAP receives and processes; the router or WAP also broadcasts its own radio signals, which each device receives when data is being sent to it. Wi-Fi currently transmits at 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz — much higher frequencies than mobile networks use, letting it carry larger amounts of data. Current Wi-Fi standards are limited to roughly 450 Mbps.

Advantages

  • Several wireless-capable devices can connect to a single router or WAP
  • High frequencies let large amounts of data be transmitted at once
  • More recent Wi-Fi standards are backward compatible with older ones
  • A fairly large range, typically around 100 m
  • Doesn't need line of sight, so it can work through obstacles

Disadvantages

  • Transfer speed falls as more devices connect to the same network
  • Less secure than a wired connection
  • Radio signals can be subject to interference

Bluetooth

Bluetooth also uses radio signals, creating a wireless connection between two and up to eight devices at once, using a frequency of 2.45 GHz. It has lower bandwidth than Wi-Fi, doesn't normally use a central access point, and is symmetrical. Devices need to be within about 10 m of each other, and the two devices requesting a connection must have matching profiles, so pointless connections can't be made.

Connecting two devices: turn Bluetooth on for both; make sure they're in range and can detect each other; set one device to search for available devices; the other device is found/made discoverable; a passcode is entered or confirmed by the user (sometimes a default passcode); both devices check/confirm the passcode; the devices are paired; and finally the frequencies/channels to use are agreed.

Advantages

  • Low power consumption
  • Doesn't need line of sight, so it can work through obstacles
  • Can be made more secure by requiring a PIN code to connect
  • Less interference, since other wireless connections use different frequencies

Disadvantages

  • Range limited to about 10 m
  • Limited to eight connected devices at a time
  • Lower bandwidth than Wi-Fi

Infrared

Infrared connections use infrared radiation. There are two types: line of sight mode, which needs a direct, unobstructed path between sender and receiver, and scatter mode, where a device broadcasts infrared into an area and any device can receive it directly or by reflection off an obstacle. The sending device needs the ability to produce an infrared signal, and the receiving device needs an infrared sensor to capture it. Infrared lasers can also transmit data in a similar way to fibre optic, but relying on the signal travelling through free space rather than a fibre, and still need a direct line of sight.

Advantages

  • The most secure form of wireless connection
  • Low cost to manufacture and buy
  • Low power consumption
  • No interference from radio frequencies

Disadvantages

  • Can't pass through obstacles
  • Line-of-sight mode needs a direct, unobstructed path between the two devices
  • Can only connect two devices at a time
  • Short range, limited to roughly 1 m
  • May suffer interference from bright light sources

Microwave

Microwave links use a beam of radio/electromagnetic waves between antennae that must be in line of sight, using frequencies from 300 MHz to 300 GHz. Parabolic dishes with horn antennae create focused "spot" beams, and multiple antennae can send and receive signals; data is modulated onto a carrier wave (for example using orthogonal frequency division multiplexing).

  • Used by mobile networks (3G/4G/5G) and by Wi-Fi systems linking devices into a LAN
  • Used for links between base stations and satellites (uplink/downlink)
  • Used by TV and radio to send outside broadcasts back to a studio, instead of an expensive satellite link
  • Used to carry mobile phone data between masts as a backbone system
  • Used for short-range indoor links between devices
  • Used to link remote or regional telephone exchanges to a main exchange, without needing copper or fibre cabling

Advantages

  • Small, directional antennas aimed at each other can reuse the same frequencies as neighbouring links without interference, using the radio spectrum economically
  • Directional antennas give good performance with low power requirements
  • Narrow beams don't interfere with other equipment
  • Small antennas allow mobile or quickly-deployed setups, such as outside broadcasts or portable radio systems
  • Large bandwidth, able to carry more data than most other media

Disadvantages

  • Tall antennas can be environmentally intrusive or hard to install in some areas
  • Needs line of sight, so antennas typically can't be more than around 50–80 km apart, and any obstacle in the way will disrupt or allow interception of the signal
  • Weather can degrade transmission — 'rain fade' from water absorbing microwaves, or scattering caused by dust, pollen or smoke — and solar events can also interfere
  • Because most of a signal passes beyond the receiving antenna, transmissions can potentially be intercepted from behind it, or even from space by satellite, which is a security concern

Securing wireless networks

Wireless connections carry particular security risks, because they don't have the same physical protection as a wired connection — a wired network is normally confined within a building or site, often behind locks and other physical security that stop an unauthorised person reaching it at all. Two protocols aim to improve wireless security:

ProtocolPurpose
WEP (Wired Equivalent Privacy)Designed to give a wireless network a level of security comparable to a wired LAN, by encrypting data sent over the wireless connection.
WPA (Wi-Fi Protected Access)A standard required of Wi-Fi-equipped devices, offering more sophisticated encryption than WEP.

Mobile communication systems 14.11

Structure of a cellular network

  • Base stations in each cell provide the wireless connection to the end user's device
  • A core/circuit-switched network handles voice calls and text messages
  • A packet-switched network handles mobile data and internet traffic
  • The public switched telephone network (PSTN) connects everything into the global telephone network

Avoiding interference between phones

Cellular networks let huge numbers of mobile phones connect at once without interfering with each other by:

  • Using frequency/time/code division multiplexing to share frequencies between many users
  • Using spread spectrum technology, allowing multiple connections on the same set of frequencies
  • Using MIMO (multiple input, multiple output) — multiple transmit/receive antennas — to increase capacity and data flow
  • Using directional antennas at base stations to avoid interference from other towers or reflections
  • Giving adjacent cells different frequencies from their neighbours
  • Reusing the same frequencies in cells that are far enough apart, to increase overall capacity despite a limited range of frequencies

Why 4G outperforms 3G

  • A much higher data transfer rate/bandwidth, meaning faster downloads, more buffering capacity, less waiting, and faster-loading web pages — thanks to higher frequencies, MIMO, multiplex division and spread spectrum technology
  • Smoother handovers between base stations, with fewer interruptions or dropped connections
  • 4G uses packet switching alone, rather than the circuit switching still used in 3G, so data is carried as IP packets that can be multiplexed with other packets for higher overall data flow

Satellite communication systems

A communication satellite is a machine launched into orbit around the Earth, used to transmit radio waves — carrying phone calls, internet data or TV broadcasts — from one place to another. Its main components are a solar-powered battery, a transponder and various antennas.

How data is sent and received:

  • Satellites stay in orbit so they appear stationary over a chosen area; several can form a constellation, each covering a small part of the Earth's surface, and use spot beams to target a specific area or user
  • Data to be sent is converted into radio waves and transmitted from a ground station up to the satellite — this is the uplink
  • Ground stations maintain a permanent link with the satellite at all times
  • An antenna on the satellite receives the radio waves, and the transponder processes them, including boosting (amplifying) the signal
  • The satellite's antenna then transmits the radio waves back down to a ground station — the downlink
  • The receiving ground station converts the radio waves back into ordinary data and forwards it to the user

Global Positioning System (GPS)

GPS uses satellites to work out a user's current location, through a process called trilateration (sometimes loosely called triangulation).

  • Each navigation satellite constantly beams radio waves toward Earth, carrying data such as its own ID code, status information, the current date and time, and almanac data describing where every GPS satellite is at any given moment
  • A device, such as a phone, or the nearest ground station, picks up these signals
  • At least two low-orbit satellites let a 2D location (latitude and longitude) be pinpointed
  • Four or more satellites let a full 3D position (latitude, longitude and altitude) be calculated — this is trilateration
  • Three satellites can be used in a triangulation process, using three lines of position
  • The location is calculated by finding the intersection of these positions, based on timing the signals from each satellite
  • GPS can also be used for surveillance: a log of a device's GPS activity can show where it is now, or where it has previously been
