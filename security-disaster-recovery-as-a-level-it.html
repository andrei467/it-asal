Security & Disaster Recovery

How networks are attacked — botnets, backdoors and brute force — how organisations protect their data against it, and how disaster recovery planning prepares for the worst.

Network security 14.12

Botnets

A botnet is a collection of internet-connected devices, each running one or more bots, where the security of every device has been taken over by a third party. It's controlled by a "bot herder" over internet links, often using digital signatures so only that controller can direct the botnet, and communicating over standard internet protocols.

How a botnet gains unauthorised access to data:

  • Malware/a bot is installed on a device without its owner's knowledge
  • These bots act as clients, though they can also be set up peer-to-peer with the controller
  • Bots connect to each other and to the controller using ordinary internet communication protocols
  • The bot herder, at a remote location, sends commands to the bots using an infected device as a command-and-control server
  • Bots can automatically scan their environment to find ways to spread themselves to other devices
  • The controller can direct bots to log keystrokes to discover login details
  • Bots can run other malware to access files or gather data and send it back to the controller
  • Botnets can launch denial-of-service (DoS) attacks on servers, blocking legitimate access to files, data or services
  • Botnets can send spam or fraudulent emails disguised to look legitimate, from infected devices
  • Botnets can distribute spyware to gather login details or other data and send it to the controller
  • Botnets use a device's computing resources without the owner's knowledge or permission, and can compromise legitimate data storage

Other kinds of attack

A user often unknowingly downloads malware (such as a virus or spyware) disguised in a file download or a link they're tricked into clicking.

A third party may want to hack a computer to damage or steal data, often by installing a backdoor in software. A cracker may instead try a brute-force approach, trying many password combinations (sometimes using purpose-built software) to gain unauthorised access using a victim's own login details.

TermMeaning
Data destructionDeleting or removing data — for example, deleting a record from a database.
Data modificationChanging data to a different value, with the new value stored in the same location as the original — for example, changing a spreadsheet cell from 100 to 101.

Protecting data from destruction and modification

  • Security measures to detect and prevent unauthorised access to a network or its connections
  • Segmenting or zoning parts of the network/servers, to reduce how much an intruder can reach
  • Firewalls, to prevent unauthorised access or intrusion into networks or network storage
  • VPNs or other secure connections for cloud storage systems
  • Authorisation and authentication techniques for gaining access to data
  • Regular or automatic checks on data integrity, with automatic alerts if an unauthorised change is detected
  • High-level encryption (such as 256-bit), restricting access to or understanding of data, making it harder to tamper with

More broadly, individuals and organisations can use a range of preventative methods:

  • Physical methods — a barrier to entry at a building or office, CCTV to see who is trying to gain access, security guards, and alarm systems to alert the organisation to an attempt
  • Biometric data — requiring a fingerprint, iris or facial scan to access data, since biological data is unique to the user and hard to fake
  • Anti-malware, anti-virus and anti-spyware software — scans a system for malware, quarantining anything found so the user can delete it
  • Firewalls — hardware- or software-based, examining incoming and outgoing traffic against a set of rules to recognise malicious traffic
  • Access rights — only allowing access to those who genuinely need the data, often tied to the username an employee is given
  • Encryption — doesn't stop data being stolen, but makes it meaningless to a third party without the key to decrypt it

The impact of security threats

There are two common motives behind a security attack: obtaining personal data, or an act of sabotage or revenge.

Individuals need to be cautious about their personal data being accessed and stolen, since this can lead to fraud or identity theft. Organisations need to be just as cautious about their stored data being accessed, stolen, manipulated or destroyed — data is a precious asset they rely on for day-to-day functioning, and without it they may struggle to operate at all. A breach can lead to lost profits directly, or indirectly through the reputational damage of the breach becoming known.

Disaster recovery management 14.13

Disaster recovery is a planning mechanism that lets an organisation prepare for a disaster and put measures in place to minimise the risk. Before that planning can happen, possible disasters first need to be identified, through several stages:

StageWhat happens
Risk analysisA risk assessment: qualitative analysis to prioritise risks, and quantitative analysis of how likely each is and what its consequences would be. The organisation identifies what could be a potential disaster (such as a power cut, cyber attack, fire, flood, denial of access to data, theft or corruption of data, or hardware failure), who or what could be harmed, and how it could affect the organisation — for example, loss of access to premises, loss of data and skills, or loss of its ability to function. It then develops methods to control the likelihood and impact of the disaster, producing a report (and sometimes a computer simulation) of the risks.
Perpetrator analysisUsually carried out as part of risk analysis: identifying who could be a perpetrator — someone who might carry out a malicious or illegal act that leads to a disaster.
Risk testingRehearsing the plans and strategies developed during risk analysis, so employees understand exactly what to do if a disaster happens. The organisation can monitor the test and amend its plans, or correct employees' behaviour if needed. This might include testing the restoration of data, replacement of hardware, reinstallation of software, emergency evacuation of offices, and emergency data protection methods.
Quantifying the riskEvaluating each risk and its prevention method in terms of cost and time. Two key measurements matter here: maximum tolerable downtime (MTD), the longest a part of the organisation could manage without access to an essential part of the network, and recovery time objective (RTO), the estimated maximum time the organisation has to recover its network and resume operations.

Detecting threats and preventing disaster

  • Power surge protection devices, to stop a power surge damaging hardware
  • A backup generator, ready if the organisation suffers a power failure
  • Backups stored in fire- and flood-proof cases, so data isn't lost in a fire or flood
  • Policies and procedures for staff to follow, to help prevent disasters such as fire or equipment damage
  • Cloud computing used as a backup for data and, potentially, hardware resources
  • Backups made and sent off-site at regular intervals
  • Backups made on-site and automatically copied to an off-site disk
  • Backups made directly to off-site/remote/cloud servers
  • Local mirrors of systems and data, using disk protection technology such as RAID
  • Fire extinguishers and mitigation systems such as alarms
  • Anti-virus software, to protect data against corruption, loss or deletion
  • Firewalls, to prevent or control unauthorised access
  • Physical security measures, to control access by personnel
  • Important passwords and codes held by more than one person, or in secure but still accessible conditions in an emergency

Restoring after a disaster

  1. Activate the disaster recovery plan
  2. Access and assess the damage, and recover based on which parts are most critical
  3. Restore data from backups
  4. Reinstall software
  5. Replace or repair hardware
