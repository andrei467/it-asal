Cambridge AS & A Level IT · Chapter 1

Data processing and information

Raw data only becomes useful once it has meaning. This chapter covers what separates data from information, what makes information good quality, how encryption keeps data secure, how systems check data is accurate, and the different ways data gets processed.

Data and information 1.1

Data

Data is raw numbers, letters, symbols, sounds or images without meaning.

Did you know?

The word ‘data’ is plural. In English we often also say ‘a piece of data’ as well, even though data is plural — both ‘pieces of data’ and ‘a piece of data’ are correct.

Some examples of data are: P952BR, @bbcclick, 359, 23557.99. A picture with no description of what it shows is also an example of raw data.

The data P952BR could have several meanings: a product code, a postal/ZIP code, or a car registration number. Because we don't know what the data means, it's meaningless.

Tip

When answering a question such as ‘Give one item of data’, don’t try to explain what the data means — that would make it information, not data. Just give numbers, letters, symbols or images.

Information

When we give data meaning or context, it becomes information — something a person reading it will understand. We turn data into information by giving it two things.

First, we give it context, which tells us what the data item represents:

Sources of direct data

A direct data source (also called a primary source) is data collected and used for a specific purpose, and must be used for that same purpose it was collected for. The data will often be collected or requested by the individual or organisation who intends to use it; the person collecting it must know the purpose it's intended for.

For example, to find out how much a pair of shoes costs in different shops, direct sources could include visiting shops and noting the prices, visiting shop websites, or surveying shop owners.

Questionnaires are useful for collecting many responses, and need to be designed carefully to capture the information required, enable effective analysis, and be quick to complete. Online questionnaires often store data directly into a database, saving time and money.

Interviews ask questions directly to respondents, and let the interviewer ask further questions based on the answers given.

Data logging is the process of automatically collecting data through a computer's sensors, storing it for later use — for example, a scientist automatically logging river water quality to analyse afterwards.

Observation collects data directly, such as watching and counting traffic, or recording video of an event to watch back and analyse.

Sources of indirect data

An indirect data source (also called a secondary source) is data collected for one purpose but then used for something different. It may be collected by the same person who later uses it, or by someone else entirely.

For example, to find shoe prices indirectly, you could look at a sales catalogue (collected for a wide range of shoes, not just the ones you want) or till receipts (there to prove purchase, not to show the going price).

Common examples of indirect data

Weather data — no single person could collect weather data across a whole country, so large weather data centres use remote sensors worldwide to collect it in real time. They collect temperature, humidity, UV light levels, wind speed and direction, rainfall, and air pressure, which is used to predict a weather forecast.

Census data — a census counts the people in a country and asks about aspects of their lives, such as jobs, age, qualifications and health. Governments use it to plan hospitals and transport; a toy store, for instance, might use it as an indirect source to see how many young people live in a region.

Electoral registers — kept mainly so registered voters can vote, but can indirectly be used to check whether someone lives at an address, or to count how many people live in a household.

Personal information collected by businesses — if you consent when buying something or signing up online, a business may pass your details to third parties for marketing. Businesses also buy lists of email or home addresses to target their marketing — for example, a textbook company benefiting from a list of teachers' emails. Most countries require businesses to get consent before sharing a customer's data with a third party.

Research — using a library, textbook, journal or website to research something is using an indirect source: the information wasn't created specifically for your purpose.

Quality of information 1.2

The quality of information can be affected by several factors, including how accurate and complete it is.

Accuracy

Data must be accurate to be good quality. If a friend asks you to meet them at a large shopping centre but gives no further detail, you may not be able to find them — more accurate data, such as "meet me outside <name of shop> at 17:00", would help you locate each other.

Examples of inaccurate information include a decimal point in the wrong place (e.g. $90.30 instead of $903.00, making something look far cheaper than it is), spelling mistakes or the wrong words, and inaccurate context (e.g. using the wrong month's data to predict a shop's stock needs).

Relevance

Information must be relevant to its purpose. Extra information that isn't needed means the user has to search through the data to find what they actually want.

Examples of irrelevant information include being told the colour of a bus when you just need its arrival time, being told a car's rental price when you want to buy it, or a phone's user guide including instructions for assembling an electrical plug.

Age

Information must be up to date to be useful; old information is often no longer useful.

Tip

When using indirect data sources, always check when the information was produced — there may be newer information available with more up-to-date results.

Examples of out-of-date information include using 40-year-old census data to find a town's current population, or using the score from the start of a sports match when it's nearly over.

Level of detail

Good quality information has exactly the right amount of detail. Too much detail makes it hard to find what's actually needed; too little means it can't be used correctly.

For example, someone ordering a vegetarian pizza who forgets to say which base they want, or give a delivery address, hasn't given the pizza company enough information to fulfil the order. Or a rail company's automated phone message might list the arrival time at every station on a route, forcing a passenger to wait through the whole message just to hear the one time they need.

Completeness

All required information must be provided for data to be good quality; missing information means it can't be used properly. For example, a garage mechanic who gives a customer a street name but not the building number has given incomplete information — the customer may not be able to find the right place.

Encryption 1.3

Encryption is the process of encoding data so that it can't be understood or used by anyone who isn't authorised to access it. Encrypted data is called cipher text, and unencrypted data is called plain text. If a hacker intercepts encrypted data, it will appear meaningless to them unless they can decrypt it, so encryption doesn't prevent data from being intercepted — it just stops that data being of any use to an unauthorised person.

Symmetric encryption

In symmetric encryption, the same key is used to encrypt and decrypt the data. This key must be known by both the sender and the receiver.

  1. The data (plain text) is encrypted using the key, turning it into cipher text
  2. The cipher text is sent to the receiver
  3. The receiver decrypts the cipher text using the same key, turning it back into plain text

A weakness of symmetric encryption is that the key itself has to be shared between sender and receiver at some point, and if that key is intercepted while being shared, an unauthorised person could use it to decrypt the data too.

Asymmetric encryption

Asymmetric encryption uses two keys: a public key and a private key. The public key can be shared with anyone and is used to encrypt data; the private key is kept secret by its owner and is used to decrypt data.

  1. The receiver generates a public key and a private key, and shares the public key with anyone who may want to send them data (the private key is never shared)
  2. The sender uses the receiver's public key to encrypt the data
  3. The cipher text is sent to the receiver
  4. The receiver uses their own private key to decrypt the cipher text back into plain text

Because the private key is never shared or transmitted, asymmetric encryption avoids the key-sharing weakness of symmetric encryption — even if the public key is intercepted, it can only be used to encrypt data, not decrypt it.

Checking the accuracy of data 1.4

Validation

Validation is the process of checking that data matches acceptable rules, to make sure it's sensible. Valid data meets the rules and is stored; invalid data doesn't meet the rules and is rejected.

For example, a registration form could use a check to stop a user leaving the email address field blank, forcing them to enter something. Despite this, validation never guarantees data is actually correct — it can make sure an email address field isn't blank, but it can't check that what was entered is a real, working email address.

There are several different validation checks — different types of rule — that can be used to decide whether data is acceptable.

Presence check

A presence check makes sure data is entered at all. If data is entered, it's accepted; if not, the user sees an error message asking them to enter it. Fields marked with an asterisk (*) on a form are often required this way.

Worked example 1.01

On a ‘contact us’ form, an email address is required. A presence check would accept any of these: a, a@b, abc@bc.com, @, 372823. Not all of these are real email addresses, but they all pass the rule that something must be present.

Range check

A range check makes sure data falls within a defined range. A limit check has a single boundary — either the highest or the lowest allowed value. A range check has two boundaries: a lower and an upper one. The symbols >, <, >= and <= are used to compare against these boundaries.

Worked example 1.02
  1. To join a club you must be over 18. The lower boundary is 18, with no upper boundary, so this is a limit check: Age >= 18.
  2. Exam grades must be the letters A to E. The lower boundary is A and the upper is E (the grade must be less than F), so this is a range check: A < Grade < F.

Data within the boundaries is valid, but valid data may still not be correct — a grade of C could be entered when A should have been, and C is still valid even though it's wrong.

Type check

A type check makes sure data is of a defined data type — for example, an age must be an integer, a grade must be text with no numbers, a price must be numerical, and a date of birth must be a date. Data of the correct type still isn't guaranteed to be correct: a date of birth of 28/12/2087 is a valid date, but clearly wrong, since that year hasn't happened yet.

Length check

A length check makes sure data is a defined length, or within a range of lengths — often used with text. For example, a password must be at least six characters, a grade must be exactly one character, or a product code must be four to six characters. Data of the right length still isn't guaranteed to be correct: a six-letter code like WWDDWW would pass a length check, even if it isn't an actual product code the business sells.

Format check

A format check (sometimes called a picture check or input mask) makes sure data matches a defined pattern.

Worked example 1.03

An email address must include an @ symbol, preceded by at least one character and followed by more characters. john@bldef.co, a@b.dek and fdc@jb would all be valid by this rule. Valid data still isn't guaranteed to be correct — fdc@jb meets the pattern but isn't a genuine address.

Lookup check

A lookup check tests whether data exists in a list. For example, a qualification might be graded pass (P), merit (M), distinction (D) or fail (F), so a validation rule could check that only P, M, D or F is entered.

Consistency check

A consistency check compares data in one field against data already held in another field in the same record, to see whether the two are consistent with each other.

Worked example 1.04

A system that records store discounts won't let a discount be entered unless the same record already shows that person has a store membership number.

Check digit

A check digit is a number (or letter) added to the end of an identification number. It's a form of redundancy check, because the digit itself isn't needed for the identification number — it only exists for validation. Check digits are commonly used in barcodes, seen on price labels, car parts and invoices.

When an identification number is first created, an algorithm (a series of calculations) generates its check digit. When the number is later entered, the same algorithm runs again, and the result should match the stored check digit. If it matches, the data is valid; if it doesn't, the data is invalid. Different systems use different calculations, but the same calculation must always be used both to create and to check a given check digit.

Worked example 1.05 — the UPC/ISBN check digit algorithm

The algorithm for a 13-digit barcode works like this:

  1. Add all the digits in even-numbered positions together.
  2. Multiply that result by 3.
  3. Add all the digits in odd-numbered positions together.
  4. Add the results of steps 2 and 3 together.
  5. Divide that result by 10.
  6. Take the remainder (modulo 10) of that division.
  7. Subtract that remainder from 10.

Valid example: the ISBN 978095734041‑1 has a check digit of 1. Running the algorithm on 978095734041: even-position digits 7+0+5+3+0+1 = 16; 16 × 3 = 48; odd-position digits 9+8+9+7+4+4 = 41; 48+41 = 89; 89 ÷ 10 = 8.9; 89 MOD 10 = 9; 10 − 9 = 1. The result, 1, matches the check digit — the ISBN is valid.

Invalid example: two digits are accidentally transposed, giving 978095374041‑1. Even-position digits now sum to 7+0+5+7+0+1 = 20; 20 × 3 = 60; odd-position digits sum to 9+8+9+3+4+4 = 37; 60+37 = 97; 97 ÷ 10 = 9.7; 97 MOD 10 = 7; 10 − 7 = 3. The result, 3, does not match the check digit of 1 — the ISBN entered is invalid.

Verification and validation

Verification is the process of checking that data entered matches its original source — unlike validation, which only checks data is of the right type or format. For example, when entering a date of birth into a database: verification might ask you to type the date a second time to make sure you typed it correctly; validation would just check that what you typed has a day, a month and a year.

Visual checking

A visual check needs a person to read both the source data and the newly entered data, and check they match. If they match, the check has passed; if not, it has failed and the data needs re-entering. However, if the original source itself was wrong, visual checking can't catch that — for example, if a sales assistant wrote $500.00 on a paper invoice when they meant $5,000.00, double-checking the computer entry against that same incorrect invoice wouldn't find the mistake.

Double data entry

In double data entry, data is entered into the system twice, and the computer compares the two entries. If they match, the data is verified; if they differ, one of the entries must have been wrong. This is why changing a password usually asks you to type the new password twice. It's still possible to pass this check with incorrect data — if the same mistake, such as a spelling error, is made both times, the two entries will still match.

Hash total

A hash total compares the sum of the values in one field against the sum of the same field in the original data. Before data entry, someone manually adds up the values for all the records; once entry is complete, the computer adds up the same field automatically and compares the two totals. If they differ, a mistake was made somewhere — either during entry or in the manual calculation.

Worked example 1.06 — a hash total catching an error

Three students' coursework marks are 74, 38 and 82. The hash total calculated by hand beforehand is 74 + 38 + 82 = 194. When entered, the marks are typed as 74, 83 and 82 — a mistake. The computer's total comes to 239, which doesn't match 194, so an error is flagged. The mistake was candidate 18264's mark: 38 was entered as 83.

Worked example 1.07 — a hash total missing an error

The same three marks (74, 38, 82) give a hash total of 194. This time they're mistakenly entered as 73, 39 and 82. The computer's total is still 73 + 39 + 82 = 194 — matching the expected total, even though two of the three marks were entered incorrectly. The errors cancelled each other out, so the hash total didn't catch them.

Control total

A control total works the same way as a hash total, but — unlike a hash total, which can be calculated from any field and may have no real meaning on its own — a control total has a genuinely useful meaning, such as the total number of items in a stock order, or the total value of items on an order.

Parity check

A parity check finds errors introduced while transmitting data. Taking a single byte as an example: one bit is used as the parity bit, and the other seven bits carry the actual data. There are two kinds: even parity, where the total number of 1s in the byte must come out even, and odd parity, where it must come out odd. The parity bit itself is set to whichever value (0 or 1) makes that true.

Worked example 1.08 — even parity

The 7 data bits 1 0 0 1 1 0 0 contain three 1s — an odd number. For even parity, the parity bit is set to 1, giving 1 0 0 1 1 0 0 1. The receiver adds up the 1s in the byte received; if the total is even, the data is accepted.

Worked example 1.09 — odd parity catching an error

The same 7 bits, 1 0 0 1 1 0 0, again contain three 1s — already odd — so for odd parity the parity bit is set to 0, giving 1 0 0 1 1 0 0 0. Suppose the byte actually received is 1 1 0 1 1 0 0 0: that has four 1s, an even number. Since odd parity was expected, the system knows an error occurred — here, the second bit was flipped from 0 to 1 in transmission.

A parity check only detects that an error occurred — it doesn't correct it, so the data has to be sent again. It can reliably catch a single flipped bit, but not every error.

Worked example 1.10 — a parity check missing an error

The 8-bit byte 1 0 0 1 1 0 0 1 is sent using even parity. It's received as 0 1 0 1 1 0 0 1 — the first two bits were both flipped. Because two bits changed, the total number of 1s is still even, so the even parity check accepts the data without noticing the error.

Checksum

A checksum is the result of a calculation performed on an entire file's contents, used to check whether that file was transmitted or copied correctly — and to check a hacker hasn't disguised a malicious file as a genuine one, since any slight change to a file produces a different checksum. A checksum is usually shown as hexadecimal digits (0–9 and A–F), with two hex digits representing one byte. Popular checksum algorithms include SHA-256, SHA-1 and MD5. If the checksum calculated before sending a file doesn't match the one calculated after, a transmission error occurred.

Although rare, MD5 and SHA-1 have been known to produce collisions — the same checksum generated for two different files — which could in theory let a hacker disguise a malicious file as a genuine one. This is very unlikely to happen by chance, so MD5 and SHA-1 remain suitable for catching ordinary transmission errors. No collisions have been reported for SHA-256, which is currently considered the safest of the three.

The need for both validation and verification

It's possible to enter valid data that is still incorrect, and it's possible to verify data that is incorrect. Using both validation and verification together reduces the chance of incorrect data being stored: if invalid-but-plausible data slips past a validation check, a verification check is more likely to catch it.

Worked example 1.11

A test result must be a single letter: F, P, M or D. The letter N is entered — this passes validation (it's a single letter) but isn't an accepted grade. When verified by double entry, the user types N the first time and M the second time; since they don't match, verification catches the problem. However, if the user had typed N both times, both validation and verification would have failed to catch the error.

Data processing 1.5

Data processing is any activity that manipulates or carries out operations on data, including collection and storage, editing and updating, sorting and searching, and output and sharing. There are three types of processing: batch, online and real-time.

Batch processing

In batch processing, operations or transactions are stored in a long queue rather than processed immediately. The whole batch is processed automatically, all at once, at a later time — typically when immediate processing isn't needed. For example, a hard disk backup can run overnight, or employee wages can be processed at midnight. This avoids using CPU resources during busy periods, keeping the system responsive for other users at peak times. It suits systems where processing doesn't need to happen immediately for the business to keep running — similar to only running a washing machine once there's a full load, overnight when electricity may be cheaper.

Other examples of batch processing include preparing utility bills, updating credit and debit card accounts, updating customer account information, preparing a weekly weather forecast, and preparing and installing software updates.

Master and transaction files

A master file usually stores data about a person, place or object — for example, a table of employee or customer records. A transaction file stores data about an event, such as an order, electricity usage, or travel expenses, and is used to update a master file.

Worked example 1.12 — 1.14

A business stores each customer's name, address and total spend in a master file, with one record per customer. Whenever a customer orders something, that order is recorded separately in a transaction file.

At regular intervals, the whole batch in the transaction file is processed: it's first validated (invalid transactions move to an errors file), then sorted into the same order as the master file (e.g. by Customer_Id). The batch process then works through the transaction file, finding the matching master file record for each transaction by its Customer_Id, generating invoices and delivery notes, and updating that customer's Total_Spent field in the master file — continuing until every transaction has updated the new version of the master file.

One way of describing the transaction processing as an algorithm:

Algorithm — batch transaction processing
For each line in transaction file
   Repeat
      Read next record in master file
   Until transaction file ID matches master file ID
   master file total for current record = master file total + transaction file value
Next line in transaction file

Advantages and disadvantages of batch processing

Advantages

  • A single automated process, needing little human involvement, which can reduce costs
  • Can be scheduled for times of low demand on computer resources, such as overnight
  • Being automated, it avoids the transcription and update errors a human operator might introduce
  • Fewer repetitive tasks for human operators

Disadvantages

  • There's a delay, since data isn't processed until the scheduled time
  • Only data of the same type can be processed, since the same automated process is applied to all of it
  • Errors can't be corrected until the whole batch process has finished

Online processing

We use online processing every day, whenever we search the internet or buy something online. It's also used to transfer money between bank accounts — for example, making a payment through a mobile banking app — and to pay for goods bought online, where a customer's debit card details are processed and payment moves from their account to the retailer's.

One method of online processing deals with data as individual transactions: a small amount of data is input, processed, and only then does the next transaction begin. An airline booking system, for example, collects all the data about a customer, flight and seat in one transaction, processes it, and issues a ticket as output. Because each transaction is processed in turn, a seat can't be double-booked — once allocated, it can't be booked again, since transactions are handled strictly in order. Concert ticket booking systems work the same way.

Electricity usage needs regular meter readings so accurate bills can be calculated. These can be taken interactively — an employee reading the meter with a handheld device, a homeowner entering the reading on the electricity company's website, or phoning it in — or automatically, using a smart meter that needs no user interaction at all.

Electronic funds transfer

Electronic funds transfer is the electronic transfer of money to or from a bank account.

MethodHow it works
Direct paymentsMoney is sent from one bank account to another, either immediately or within a given time frame. A time-framed transfer is usually processed overnight as a batch of direct payments.
ATMs (automated teller machines)Used to withdraw cash. The ATM looks up the customer's account, and if there are enough funds, allows the withdrawal and deducts it from the balance.
Debit cardsLinked to a bank account. The customer confirms the card is theirs with a PIN, or via contactless payment (without a PIN, up to a transaction limit). The bank checks the balance is sufficient, confirms funds are available to the retailer, then creates the transaction: subtracting the amount from the customer's account and adding it to the retailer's.
Direct debitsUsed for regular payments, such as utility bills that vary each month. The account holder authorises an organisation to take payments automatically.

Automatic stock control

A stock control system keeps a shop's stock within set limits, using a database that stores data about each product, its supplier, and the quantity left in stock, including a reorder level (the stock level that triggers a new order) and a reorder amount (how much to order each time).

Each purchase at the till scans a barcode; the system looks the product up in the database and reduces its stock level by one. When stock reaches the reorder level, the system automatically orders the reorder amount. When that stock arrives, the database is updated with the quantity received.

Worked example 1.15

Stock data shows four black polo shirts in stock, with a reorder level of three. The next time one is sold, stock drops to three, triggering a reorder of ten more. Separately, only one pair of casual trainers is in stock — already below its reorder level — so four pairs are presumably already on order; when they arrive, the stock figure increases by four.

Real stock control systems are more complex: they track stock both in the shop and in an on-site stockroom, adjust quantities when unsold perishable items are removed from sale, and account for stock lost to damage or theft. Shops carry out regular stocktakes, comparing the physical stock against the database so any discrepancies can be corrected.

Electronic data interchange (EDI) and B2B

Electronic data interchange (EDI) is a standardised format for exchanging data between organisations — for example, a school submitting coursework marks and estimated grades to an awarding body. Application program interfaces (APIs) give a standard way for different software to import and export data; accounts software, for instance, may use an API to send tax data to the government.

A traditional, paper-based way of ordering stock from a supplier: the buyer creates and posts a paper purchase order; the supplier enters it into their system, prints a delivery note to send with the stock, and prints an invoice to send to the buyer; the buyer receives the stock and invoice and arranges payment.

Business-to-business (B2B) EDI replaces this with an electronic order sent directly into the supplier's ordering system, which can give immediate electronic confirmation, receipt and updates. An electronic invoice is matched against the original order, and payment can be made by electronic funds transfer once the buyer confirms the goods arrived — saving time and money, with no printing or posting needed, and letting both sides see exactly what's happening to the order at any point.

Online shopping

Online stores use an interactive system where customers add products to a virtual shopping basket and pay using electronic funds transfer. The store typically sends a confirmation email, and may follow up with text messages updating the customer on delivery.

Real-time processing

A real-time processing system processes data as soon as it's input, generating output instantly — used whenever data needs to be updated immediately.

Microprocessor-controlled / computer-controlled systems

Many such systems use their own outputs as inputs, forming a continuous feedback loop that lets the system monitor the effect of its own actions and adapt as conditions change.

SystemReal-time processingHow output affects input
GreenhousesSensors detect temperature or soil moisture.Inputs trigger outputs such as lights, watering or heaters; the system then monitors the result of those outputs.
Central heatingA processor detects the temperature.Turning the heater on raises the temperature, which is then monitored as a new input, so the system doesn't let it get too hot.
Air conditioningA processor detects the temperature.Turning the air conditioning on lowers the temperature, which is monitored as a new input, so it doesn't get too cold.
Burglar alarmsSensors (laser beams, motion, temperature) detect entry.A changed sensor reading can indicate an intruder, triggering an alarm as the output.
Smart motorwaysCameras monitor traffic flow.Heavy traffic may lower the speed limit; the effect of that new limit is then measured as a further input.
Car park barriersSensors detect cars entering and leaving.Each entry reduces the spaces available; each exit frees one up, letting another car in.
Traffic lightsMotion or pressure sensors detect waiting cars.Detecting waiting traffic changes the lights from red to green to manage flow.

Wireless sensor and actuator networks

Sensors can also be wireless, transmitting data straight to a microprocessor with no physical connection — useful where cabling could be damaged, or where weight needs to be saved, since cables can be heavy. They can control actuators (motors that move or operate another device) as outputs, in the same way as a wired system. Examples include smart homes, guidance systems and autonomous vehicles.

Smart homes — a householder might set their heating to switch on if the temperature falls below 20°C. The system constantly monitors the temperature; when it falls below 20°C the boiler turns on, generating heat that raises the temperature — the output (heat) affecting the input (temperature) — and once it rises back above 20°C, the boiler turns off again, and the cycle repeats as a continuous feedback loop.

Draw a feedback loop diagram to show how an air-conditioning system would work, in the same way as the central heating example above.

Guidance systems for space rockets — the delay in sending data to a ground control centre, and waiting for a response, can be far too long for a rocket's guidance needs, so real-time processing lets the rocket react immediately to its own sensors — for example, cutting thrust if an engine overheats, or adjusting course if its direction is wrong — while still transmitting data back for people to monitor. Wireless sensors let sensors be placed in places cables might be damaged, such as near engines or inside fuel tanks.

Autonomous vehicles — self-driving cars, aircraft, drones and ships all rely on real-time processing to react immediately to hazards such as other vehicles, pedestrians, animals or weather; any delay would put them at risk of an accident. Wireless sensors let the vehicle measure things like height, speed, traffic and fuel, feeding directly into the microprocessor's calculations, while also letting people monitor the vehicle remotely.
