The Digital Divide

This page covers what the digital divide is, what causes it, and how it affects different groups — by age, location, education, income, accessibility and how industrially developed a country is — along with the ways it can be reduced.

What is the digital divide? 6.1

The digital divide is the difference in ability to access modern technology. It refers to the differences in people's ability to access technology, such as smart phones, personal computers, tablets, laptops and TV, and the information and communications services they provide. A digital divide exists between those who have full access to technology and those who have restricted or no access.

There can be a digital divide between countries, between different demographic groups (particular sections of the population) and between different economic areas (areas defined by their production and consumption of goods and services). It can be judged by several factors, such as a person's age, income and location. The digital divide is a complex and growing issue.

Why it matters

Information technology generally brings many benefits. You can interact with your bank account from a smart phone, place orders for goods using a computer and the internet, and access knowledge and learning from a wide range of places using the internet. A lack of access to technology is seen as a disadvantage. It may lead to inequality: people who cannot access technology may miss out on opportunities, both in their personal lives and in the workplace.

For a country, the digital divide may describe the difference between those who have regular access to modern technology and those who do not. At an international level, it may describe the difference between countries with access to the latest technology and those with less access.

Challenges of technology

Digital technology also has challenges, and some people may choose to avoid it. For example, some people might not trust the development of artificial intelligence (AI) — the use of computer programs that can simulate human intelligence. Other people might experience a negative effect on their health because of social media.

Causes and effects of the digital divide 6.2

The digital divide describes the gap between people who have the latest technology and those who do not. There are many causes.

Access to electricity

Technology requires electricity. Devices can run on battery or directly from mains electricity, but even a battery needs recharging. It is difficult and expensive to install electrical cables in remote parts of the world, many hundreds of miles from power stations, such as deserts, mountains and remote villages or towns. Solar power can help reduce the impact of being in a remote place, but it is expensive to install. Without electricity, people cannot use digital devices effectively.

Cost of technology

New technology is very expensive. Old technology is cheaper but will not perform the same functions, access new programs or be as reliable. People from poorer areas may not be able to afford new technology, the electricity to power it, or repairs if it breaks. Cost can also create a digital divide in big cities: cities have shops that sell new technology, but people in low-paid jobs may not be able to afford to buy or rent it.

Ability to repair devices

Digital devices often need repairing or upgrading — for example adding storage or updating software. If a person cannot find a way to repair their device, such as a local computer or phone technician, they cannot use it. A person with a particular brand of mobile device may not be able to get it repaired locally, which may mean they need to buy a new device.

Access to the internet and wireless connections

Many services are only available using the internet, such as video conferencing, email/messaging apps and websites. Things like using word processing apps and printing may not need the internet. Remote places make it difficult to install internet connections:

  • Mobile phone networks and satellites remove the need for physical connections
  • Satellite connections cost a lot to use, and can be slow and unreliable — the signals travel through the atmosphere into space and back, so they can be disrupted by weather such as thunderstorms
  • Mobile phone networks only have a limited range and may not reach remote locations

Very slow connections, or a total lack of connection, add to the digital divide. Lack of internet is probably one of the biggest factors.

Availability of technology

Availability refers to how many digital devices there are compared with how many people want them. For example, if 10 000 people can afford a device, have internet access and can use it, but only 5000 devices are available, then 5000 people still cannot access a digital device.

  • The COVID-19 pandemic disrupted manufacturing. Prices of digital devices increased significantly, because the microchips needed were not produced in the numbers needed.
  • New technology may need new manufacturing processes or better robots to build, which may limit the quantity of high-performance technology available and increase its price.

Groups affected by the digital divide 6.3

The digital divide can be compared between:

  • People in different age groups
  • People in cities and people in rural areas
  • People with differing levels of education
  • People in different socio-economic groups
  • People with accessibility barriers
  • People in more and less industrially developed nations

Different age groups

Technology is developing at great speed, with new technology released every year. People born in the 1960s grew up with very limited information technology: landline telephones, letters typed or handwritten and sent by post or fax machine, and goods bought at the shops. People born in the 1980s saw new technology emerging: simple mobile telephones, short text messages, basic low-speed internet access using telephone lines, simple websites and downloading items from file storage. By the start of the 21st century, wireless technology and almost instant communication arrived — smart phones with instant access to online banking and shopping, booking healthcare appointments, social media, messaging, email and streaming.

Many people today were taught the IT skills in school or college, and younger people are better able to learn new skills as features are introduced. Some older people may find it hard to learn new skills because the technology works in a very different way from what they grew up with.

A person's ability to learn about and use technology effectively is called digital literacy — the skill level of a person to use digital technology to find and create information and to communicate with others. A lack of digital literacy may prevent a person from benefiting from online services and can affect any age group. An older person in a rural area may lack it because there is no local community college; a younger person may lack it because they cannot afford technology and are less confident using it.

Real-life example: Mohammed

Mohammed is a 75-year-old with a mobile phone he only uses to phone his daughter. His hospital used to send letters reminding him of appointments but now sends text messages asking him to confirm by text. He does not know how to send a text, and does not want to bother his busy daughter, so he worries he will lose his appointment.

Reducing the divide between age groups. One of the biggest barriers is fear of new things; people are more comfortable with familiar ways. Some believe they are not clever enough, and older people often feel they 'cannot keep up'. People are more likely to learn when the benefits are greater than the challenge — for example social media can allow communication with family and friends and combat loneliness. Many countries support older people to appreciate and learn technology.

Did you know?

In a 2021 survey, 75% of US adults in the 65+ age group said they used the internet, compared with 99% in the 18–29 age group.

  • Retirement villages use sensors to monitor a patient's health, and doctors use high-speed broadband for video-conference check-ins
  • Libraries and community centres offer short IT sessions to help people get started with computers, tablets and the internet

Cities and rural areas

Where a person lives affects their access to technology. High-speed and reliable internet is more often found in cities than small villages and towns, because the infrastructure (the facilities needed for the operation of a society, such as roads, buildings and utilities) is far more developed in cities. Many people live close together, so there is greater demand and it takes less resource to connect them (for example 300 people with 100 m of cable), which makes it cheaper for providers. In rural areas, a connection over a large distance for a small number of people is not very profitable, so providers may avoid investing there.

People without high-speed access may struggle to use services such as online shopping and online TV, and may feel isolated if they cannot stream films or TV reliably. In rural locations mobile network connection may be limited or absent.

Real-life example: Sofia

Sofia is a furniture designer in a rural area of Spain who works from home and sends designs to clients online. Her internet connection does not allow effective video-conferencing, which puts her at a disadvantage compared with designers who have high-speed internet.

Reducing the effects in rural areas. There are two main issues: access to digital devices and technology, and how they connect to the internet (via the internet or mobile networks). Building infrastructure can be very challenging — a village could be hundreds of kilometres from a city or up in the mountains. One way to provide access is satellite technology, which reduces the infrastructure needed.

Real-life example: BharatNet

BharatNet is an Indian government initiative to provide affordable internet in rural India, by increasing fibre optic cabling infrastructure and by delivering access through satellites in approximately 5300 locations.

Differing levels of education

Using technology can be complex: different technologies have different interfaces and are used in different ways. Without a certain level of education in how to use it, it can seem challenging. Many businesses have online systems to buy products, book tickets or use services. People who are not educated in using them may find it frustrating and miss out on opportunities. Reasons include no access (so no experience), no interest, or struggling to keep learning and giving up.

Did you know?

In the UK, 6.3% of adults said they had never used the internet in 2020 — an estimated 3.4 million people.

Real-life example: concert tickets

A person wants tickets for their favourite band, which are only available online. They usually phone the venue, but that option is unavailable. By the time they work out the online system, the tickets have sold out.

Reducing the divide by improving digital literacy. One effective way is digital literacy teaching through technology courses in local communities, offered by colleges, schools and libraries, free or at a low cost. People learn alongside others of the same skill level, which helps them feel comfortable. Local face-to-face courses have a teacher or leader to help with problems, and people often find them more useful because they may need skills just to access online courses.

Real-life example: a 12-week course

A country offered a 12-week course in everyday software, provided by a virtual university, covering basic software use and using the internet for communication. It helped people feel comfortable and confident, and the country believed it made them more competitive in the digital world.

Different socio-economic groups

The divide between socio-economic groups often revolves around cost. Richer people can afford higher-speed internet and expensive advancements in technology, which improves their standard of living through better access to information and learning, entertainment, online services such as shopping, and worldwide communication. Lack of access to information tends to have a greater impact on people with lower levels of education. Lack of access to services such as free education can create a greater divide between the rich and the poor, so ensuring access for all to education and information is an important way of improving socio-economic circumstances.

Real-life example: Taliya and Jaz

Taliya, a film studies student, has high-quality recording equipment, a 4K monitor and a high-spec computer. Jaz is also a film studies student but only has a low-spec mobile phone to film and edit his projects, and knows he could produce better work with a better computer.

Reducing the divide. Many countries try to provide technology at lower cost (or free) to poorer communities. Large organisations such as Facebook and Google work with internet service providers to build infrastructure to bring internet access to areas that cannot afford it, for example by reducing the costs of installing Wi-Fi access points. They collaborate because more people with internet access may use their services.

Accessibility barriers

The digital divide can affect people with specific challenges in accessing a technology — for example, someone with vision loss cannot use a digital display effectively. Many devices include support: screen readers convert the text on the screen into sound, so a person hears what is on the screen, and voice input/commands help those who cannot operate keyboards or touchscreens effectively. Support for people with accessibility barriers is important when digital technology is developed.

More and less industrially developed nations

The USA, Europe and northern Asia have greater access to modern technology; these areas mostly consist of more industrially developed countries. Access is more restricted in parts of Africa, India and southern Asia, which are less industrially developed. These countries may need to use their financial resources to provide other important services, and the costs of building technology infrastructure are very high. However, lacking technology and the internet could put them at a competitive and economic disadvantage: it may limit trade opportunities, so they fall behind financially, and it may affect the level of education provided and the skill level of citizens, preventing them from competing internationally.

Real-life example: a Kenyan village cooperative

A village cooperative in Kenya makes hand-beaded necklaces and sets up an online business to reach a wider market, but poor internet access means it cannot run the website effectively. Communities may create campaigns to the local or national government for better services, possibly with a consultation with government IT specialists, so a plan can balance technology and costs against the impact on the cooperative.

Reducing the divide in less industrially developed areas. The internet has become essential to global infrastructures. Satellite technology may help remote areas. Governments promote the businesses and products they could offer with better infrastructure and are keen for more developed countries to invest and help build it.

Did you know? Starlink

Starlink aims to provide high-speed internet to the world using satellites that beam internet signals to Earth, reaching places where cables and other wireless connections cannot. The challenge is that satellite technology is expensive to run, so the cost of access is quite high compared with ground-based access.

Real-life example: Microsoft Airband Initiative

Microsoft aims to close the global digital divide by working with partners to provide internet access to millions of people with limited or no access, using a range of wired and wireless technologies. One part uses Television White Spaces (TVWS), a fixed wireless technology that uses often unused and unlicensed radio frequencies that currently broadcast television, giving access in countries where ground-based infrastructure is very expensive or slow to build.
